## Guideline 1: Getting Started with Python

### 1.1 Before Python

Before you write a single line of Python, it helps to know what a computer actually understands and how far removed that is from the words you are about to type.

At the very bottom sits **binary**: sequences of 0s and 1s, the only "language" a processor natively responds to. A single 0 or 1 is a **bit**; group eight of them together and you get a **byte**, the basic unit most systems use to represent a character, a small number, or a fragment of something larger. Every instruction the processor carries out, every number stored in memory, every letter that eventually renders on your screen is, underneath, nothing more than a pattern of these electrical high and low states. There is no text in a computer's memory in the way you experience text. There are only patterns, and everything above this layer exists to give those patterns meaning a person can work with.

Writing software directly in binary is technically possible and practically miserable, so early programmers built a thin layer of shorthand on top of it: **assembly language**. Assembly replaces long strings of binary with short mnemonic codes, things like `MOV` to move a value, `ADD` to add two numbers, or `JMP` to jump to a different point in the program, that map almost one-to-one onto the processor's raw instructions. Assembly is faster to write than binary and considerably easier to read, but it is still tied tightly to the specific hardware it runs on. Assembly written for one processor family will not run on another without being substantially rewritten, and the programmer is still required to think in terms of registers, memory addresses, and individual instructions rather than in terms of the actual problem being solved.

**High-level languages** exist to close that gap. Instead of writing instructions for a specific chip, you write instructions that read closer to structured English and mathematics, and a separate program (a compiler or an interpreter, a distinction covered in the next section) translates that into the binary the machine actually needs. Languages like FORTRAN and COBOL, developed in the 1950s, were among the first attempts at this, followed later by C, which became one of the most influential languages in computing history and directly shaped how many later languages, Python included, structured their thinking around functions, variables, and control flow. Python sits much further along this same spectrum than C does. Where C still asks you to manage memory directly and think in relatively close proximity to the hardware, Python is deliberately designed to be read and written the way a person reasons through a problem. A single line of Python, such as `sorted(my_list)`, can expand into dozens of lower-level instructions on your behalf: comparisons, memory allocation, loop control, all handled invisibly so that the line you actually write stays focused on intent rather than mechanism.

Python is also **open source**. Its source code is publicly available, maintained by the Python Software Foundation and an enormous volunteer community, and free for anyone to use, study, modify, or redistribute under a permissive license. This stands in contrast to proprietary languages and platforms whose internal workings are closed off and controlled entirely by a single company. Open source matters practically, not just ideologically: because anyone can inspect Python's source, build a library on top of it, and share that library freely, the language accumulated an enormous ecosystem of contributed tools far faster than a closed, centrally controlled language typically could. Much of what makes Python useful today, its libraries for data analysis, web development, and machine learning among them, exists because the language itself was never locked behind a paywall or a single vendor's roadmap.

One clarification worth stating plainly, since the name invites the wrong image: **Python is not named after the snake.** It is named after the British comedy troupe *Monty Python's Flying Circus*, a favorite of the language's creator. The influence runs deeper than the name alone. Python's official documentation and tutorials have historically used "spam" and "eggs" as placeholder variable names, a direct reference to a famous Monty Python sketch, instead of the more generic "foo" and "bar" used in many other languages. The logo occasionally features a snake as a visual pun, and plenty of Python conferences and communities have leaned into the reptilian branding since, but the name itself, and a fair amount of the culture around it, traces back to comedy, not zoology.

### 1.2 History of Python

Python was created by **Guido van Rossum**, a Dutch programmer working at the Centrum Wiskunde & Informatica (CWI) research institute in the Netherlands. He began developing it in December 1989, reportedly as a personal project to occupy his time over the Christmas holidays, and released the first public version, 0.9.0, in **February 1991**.

Van Rossum had previously worked on a teaching language called **ABC**, developed at CWI in the 1980s with the explicit goal of being easy for beginners to learn. ABC never achieved wide adoption, largely because it was difficult to extend with new capabilities and poorly suited to the kind of systems-level programming real-world software often required. Python was, in many ways, van Rossum's second attempt at the same underlying goal, informed by what had gone wrong the first time. He kept ABC's emphasis on readability and approachability but built the implementation closer to **C**, and deliberately made the language extensible, so that new functionality could be added through modules rather than requiring changes to the language itself. The result was a language that asked programmers to spend less mental effort on syntax, punctuation, and boilerplate, and more effort on the actual logic of the problem being solved. This philosophy was later distilled into a short set of guiding principles known as *The Zen of Python*, accessible in any Python interpreter by typing `import this`, which includes lines like "Simple is better than complex" and "Readability counts."

Python's version history is also marked by a significant fork in the road: the transition from **Python 2.x to Python 3.x**. Python 1.0 was released in 1994, and Python 2.0 followed in October 2000, becoming the version that carried Python into widespread industry adoption through the 2000s. As the language matured, however, its maintainers identified design inconsistencies that could not be cleanly fixed without breaking backward compatibility, most notably in how Python 2 handled text: strings and byte data were often treated interchangeably in ways that caused subtle bugs, especially once software began dealing routinely with non-English text and web data. Python 3.0, released in **December 2008**, resolved this by treating text and byte data as genuinely distinct types from the ground up, along with a number of smaller cleanups (the `print` statement became a proper function, integer division behavior changed, and several redundant ways of doing the same thing were removed). The catch was that Python 3 code was not fully compatible with Python 2 code, and existing Python 2 codebases, along with the libraries they depended on, could not simply be switched over without real migration work. This meant the two versions coexisted, and often directly competed for developer attention and library support, for well over a decade. Python 2 was officially retired on **January 1, 2020**, meaning it no longer receives security updates or maintenance, and Python 3 is now the sole actively developed version and the standard for all new work.

The **Python Software Foundation (PSF)**, a nonprofit organization, was established in 2001 to hold Python's intellectual property and oversee the language's continued open development, separating its governance from any single company or individual. Since then, Python's growth has accelerated well beyond its original teaching-language ambitions. Its rise is closely tied to the emergence of fields that exploded in the same period: data science and machine learning found a natural home in Python through libraries like NumPy, Pandas, and later TensorFlow and PyTorch; web development adopted it through frameworks like Django and Flask; and automation and scripting tasks across nearly every industry defaulted to Python because it was quick to write and easy to read even for non-specialists. Python now consistently ranks at or near the top of major language popularity indexes, such as the TIOBE Index and the Stack Overflow Developer Survey, and it is often cited as one of the most commonly taught first languages in university computer science programs, precisely because the qualities van Rossum designed it around, readability and low syntactic overhead, make it as approachable for a beginner as it is capable for a professional.

### 1.3 Three Characteristics of Python

Python is often introduced through three defining characteristics. Understanding them now, even briefly, will make everything else in this book easier to reason about, because nearly every later topic, from how errors are reported to how libraries are structured, traces back to one of these three properties.

**High-Level.** As covered above, Python abstracts away the details of memory management, hardware instructions, and low-level data representation. You write code that describes *what* you want to happen, and Python's underlying implementation handles *how* it happens on the actual machine. Consider the difference in effort between languages. In C, printing text to the screen requires including a specific library, writing a formatted string with explicit format specifiers, and managing the underlying character buffer correctly. In Python, the equivalent is one line:

```python
print("Hello, world!")
```

Nothing about memory allocation, character encoding, or output buffering is your concern at this level; Python's interpreter handles it beneath the surface. This is the essence of a high-level language: complexity is not eliminated, it is relocated somewhere you do not have to look at it unless you choose to.

**Interpreted.** Some languages, like C or C++, are **compiled**: the entire program is translated into machine code all at once, before it ever runs, producing a standalone executable file that the operating system can launch directly. Python is **interpreted**: an interpreter reads and executes your code line by line, translating and running each statement as it goes, rather than translating the whole program up front into a separate file. In practice, this means you can open a Python interactive shell, type a single line such as `2 + 2`, and see the result, `4`, immediately, without writing a full program, saving a file, or running a separate compilation step. This makes Python notably slower at raw execution speed compared to a fully compiled language, since translation is happening continuously rather than once ahead of time, but it also makes development considerably faster and more forgiving. You can test a single line, inspect a variable's value mid-program, and catch mistakes without recompiling an entire codebase, which is a large part of why Python is often the language of choice for prototyping, data exploration, and teaching.

**Object-Oriented.** Python organizes code around **objects**, bundles of data and the behavior that acts on that data, rather than treating code as a plain sequence of instructions operating on separate, disconnected variables. Nearly everything in Python, including numbers, strings, and even functions, is itself an object carrying its own defined behavior. A string in Python is not just raw text; it is an object that comes with built-in behavior attached, such as:

```python
name = "python"
print(name.upper())  # PYTHON
```

Here, `.upper()` is behavior that belongs to the string object itself, not a separate function operating on the text from the outside. This matters early on because it shapes how Python code is typically structured, and it becomes essential once you start building anything larger than a short script, since real programs are usually organized as collections of custom objects (called classes) that model the entities the program deals with. Object-oriented programming is covered in full later in this book, but it is worth knowing from day one that it is not an optional add-on layered onto Python; it is part of how the language is built, down to its most basic data types.

### 1.4 Where to Learn and Code Python

You do not need expensive or specialized tools to start writing Python. What you need is somewhere to write code and something to run it, and there are several reasonable starting points depending on your situation, your budget, and how you learn best.

- **IDEs (Integrated Development Environments):** Tools like PyCharm or Jupyter Notebook combine a code editor, a way to run your code, and debugging tools in one installed application. PyCharm in particular is built specifically around Python and offers features like intelligent auto-completion, built-in error checking, and integrated project management, which becomes valuable once you are working across multiple files rather than a single short script. Jupyter Notebook takes a different approach, letting you mix code, output, and written notes in the same document, cell by cell, which makes it especially well suited to data analysis and iterative experimentation rather than building a full standalone application.

- **Google Colab:** A free, browser-based notebook environment, built on the same underlying format as Jupyter, that runs Python on Google's servers rather than your own machine, requiring no local installation at all. It is especially useful for data science and machine learning work, since it provides free access to more computing power than most personal laptops have, including optional GPU acceleration, and for anyone who wants to start coding immediately from any device without configuring an environment first.

- **VS Code:** A lightweight, highly extensible code editor developed by Microsoft that, with the official Python extension installed, becomes a capable environment for writing and running Python locally. It strikes a middle ground between a bare text editor and a full IDE: it is faster and less resource-intensive than something like PyCharm, but with extensions it can support debugging, linting, version control integration, and virtually any other language you might need alongside Python, which makes it a common choice for developers who work across multiple languages or projects.

- **University Courses:** Formal coursework, whether a dedicated introductory programming course or a broader computer science curriculum that uses Python as its teaching language, offers structured pacing, graded feedback, and the ability to ask an instructor directly when something does not make sense. This structure and accountability is valuable for learners who benefit from external deadlines and a clear, sequential curriculum rather than open-ended self-study.

- **Online Courses:** Platforms such as Coursera, edX, and freeCodeCamp offer structured Python curricula, often built by universities or experienced industry practitioners, with the flexibility to move at your own pace outside a fixed academic schedule. Many of these are free or low-cost, include hands-on coding exercises with automated feedback, and, in the case of university-affiliated platforms, can sometimes offer certificates or credentials recognized in professional settings.

- **YouTube:** A vast and mostly free resource for both structured full-course tutorials, some running many hours long and covering an entire beginner curriculum, and short, targeted explanations of a single concept you got stuck on. Its main strength is flexibility: you can search for the exact problem you are facing at the moment you are facing it, rather than working through a fixed syllabus, though the tradeoff is that quality and accuracy vary far more widely than in a curated course or a textbook.

No single option is objectively "correct," and they are not mutually exclusive. Many learners end up combining several of them, for instance, following a structured online course for the conceptual sequence while using an IDE or Colab in a second window to actually type out and run what the course is teaching, or supplementing a university course with a YouTube video whenever a lecture moves faster than the material sinks in.
